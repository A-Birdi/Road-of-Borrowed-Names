# The Road of Borrowed Names
## Harmony Cut-In Art Direction Correction

**Document type:** Formal implementation directive and visual-acceptance brief  
**Scope:** Harmony portrait artwork, appearance-aware asset construction, internal cut-in animation, and integration into the existing Harmony presentation  
**Primary visual target:** The selected compact player-and-Suzu mockup, supplied again with this directive  
**Comparison material:** The current pairing sheet, cut-in frame sequence, and `technique_suzu_normal_1280x720.webm`, where supplied

### Relationship to the existing assignment

Continue the existing project from its **actual newest local source**, preserving unfinished work and correct implementation. This directive refines the Harmony portrait **art target and production approach**. It does not replace the broader expressive-storytelling, world-idle, animated-dialogue-portrait, battle-UI, learning, or validation requirements.

Where an earlier description of “pixel art” has been interpreted as requiring the simpler, more direct portrait rendering seen in the current attempt, **the selected mockup takes precedence for the Harmony cut-ins’ finish and fidelity**. Preserve the game’s identity, but do not use the ordinary portrait system’s visual limitations as the ceiling for this special presentation layer.

The current cut-in artwork is **provisional, not visually approved**. Correct integration and recognizable characters are useful progress; they do not establish that the requested art direction has been achieved.

Do not restart unrelated tasks or interrupt another worker’s successful work to begin this correction. Preserve the current functional implementation as a comparison checkpoint, then revise the genuine remainder through the established source and build workflow.

> **The system is closer to correct than the art itself is. Preserve the working system; bring the artwork, expression, and shared performance materially closer to the chosen mockup without sacrificing player customization.**

## 1. What is currently not landing

The current Harmony implementation is a meaningful step forward, and the on-field party animation is appreciated. This is an **art-direction correction**, not a rejection of the feature or its existing choreography.

The supplied portrait implementation still reads too much like an enlarged extension of the ordinary in-engine pixel-portrait system. Relative to the selected mockup, its hair, faces, clothing, shading, and pose composition are simpler, flatter, and less emotionally energetic.

The distinction is between **showing two recognizable characters together** and **depicting two characters performing a coordinated, expressive moment**. The current work demonstrates the first more strongly than the second.

Do not interpret this feedback as “the characters are unrecognizable” or “nothing is animated.” The concern is the quality and direction of the authored drawing, and the impact of the resulting short performance. A contact sheet alone also cannot establish the smoothness or timing of the complete animation.

Retain successful technical work while treating the visual result as unfinished against the selected reference.

## 2. The target standard

The selected mockup is the **fidelity target**, not merely a suggestion for screen placement.

The final artwork does not need to copy the reference pixel for pixel. It does need to be **very close in visual richness, expression, dynamic posing, depth, and overall impact**. It should read as some of the highest-fidelity character art in the project.

The intended result is:

> **A special illustrated battle cut-in, with the chosen mockup’s expressive, richly shaded finish and a pixel-compatible presentation—not a normal dialogue portrait made larger.**

Use the mockup to establish the quality of the faces, hair, clothing, lighting, gesture, overlap, and paired composition. Use the game’s authoritative appearance data and character references to establish who is depicted and what they are wearing.

A deliberately richer illustration layer is appropriate for a special Harmony moment. Do not flatten its shading, remove its pose energy, or enlarge its pixel blocks merely to make it match the ordinary portrait renderer. At the same time, avoid introducing unrelated photorealism, glossy vector styling, or a completely different character-design language.

The game remains a tile-based Japanese-learning RPG. This correction does not authorize a global redraw of the overworld, altered map scale, redesigned battle UI, or changes to its learning systems.

## 3. What closer to the mockup means

### 3.1 Face rendering and expression

Give the faces clearer emotional intent through deliberate eye openings, eyelids, brows, highlights, mouth shape, cheek structure, and head angle. The expression should remain legible at the **actual in-battle display size**, not only when inspected in a large asset sheet.

The faces should feel performed: confident, attentive, playful, reassuring, or precise according to the participant. A tiny change to the mouth on an otherwise neutral frontal face is not sufficient for the principal held pose.

Preserve glasses, complexion, facial identity, and other established features. Improve their drawing and integration rather than removing them to simplify expression.

### 3.2 Hair rendering

Move toward the mockup’s greater volume and more varied silhouette. Build coherent overlapping locks and masses, with finer strand accents where they clarify the shape. Use intentional highlights and shadows to describe the hair’s direction and depth.

Do not substitute a uniformly striped highlight band, random texture, or isolated bright pixels for volume. Motion should carry through the main hair shapes and attached accessories without causing the silhouette to crawl or the character’s hairstyle to change between frames.

### 3.3 Clothing and accessories

Clothing should show meaningful folds, seams or construction where visible, overlap, and material separation. Shading should describe the torso and the way the garment follows the pose.

Glasses, the equipped head accessory, collars, scarves, and the satchel strap must appear integrated into that richer drawing. They cannot look like ordinary low-detail icons pasted over an otherwise high-detail illustration.

Render the equipment that is actually visible and worn. Do not invent extra armor, weapons, jewelry, or costume details simply to make the composition more elaborate.

### 3.4 Pose and paired composition

The cut-in should be designed as one cooperative composition, not two independent frontal busts placed beside one another.

Use complementary head angles, shoulder lines, gesture directions, and controlled overlap. Both participants should contribute to the same visual moment. Their energy should lead into the shared action rather than resemble a character-selection screen.

Retain the established companion-left/player-right composition and compact left-side placement. Keep both faces and important hands readable. Do not make a hand or accessory unintentionally obscure the other person’s expression.

There is no “VS” treatment, hostile face-off, opposing-side framing, or additional oversized title. This is a party rally.

### 3.5 Shading, lighting, and finish

Use consistent light direction, material-aware highlights, richer color transitions, and selective local contrast. The target is convincing form and a carefully authored finish, not noise density.

Faces, hands, hair, cloth, glass, and metal should not receive identical treatment. Preserve color coherence between the player and companion so that independently assembled assets still belong in the same illustration.

The held composition must remain attractive with decorative particles disabled. Sparkles and a dramatic backing may reinforce good artwork; they must not disguise an unresolved pose or flat rendering.

### 3.6 Evaluate at equivalent display scale

Compare the candidate and reference at a comparable **visible face size and battle footprint**. Include full-frame context as well as close crops.

A larger canvas, larger on-screen overlay, sharper export, or higher frame count is not by itself proof of improved art direction. The drawing and performance must carry the improvement.

## 4. Customization must not lower the art target

The player’s customization is a firm requirement, not permission to settle for visibly lower-fidelity player art than companion art.

The production goal is to support both:

- high-fidelity, authored Harmony artwork;
- the player’s actual supported appearance and worn equipment.

Treat the player cut-in as a **layered illustrated kit**, not one fixed illustration of the reference character and not a crude reconstruction of the ordinary sprite.

A fixed green-clothed, auburn-haired player can be used as the first visual proof. It is not a complete implementation. The final system must retain the existing appearance choices rather than silently restricting character creation or replacing unsupported looks with that example.

Do not add new customization categories for this work. Read and support the categories the current project actually provides.

## 5. Required production approach for the player

### 5.1 Inventory the actual appearance system

Before locking the asset specification, inspect the current appearance, equipment, and cosmetic registries. Record the real identifiers and the resolver that determines the player’s effective worn look.

Distinguish base customization from equipped overrides and identify which components are visible in the intended crop. The inventory must come from the newest source, not assumptions based on one screenshot.

This directive does not supply or claim to have extracted that registry. Producing it accurately is part of the implementation or external-art handoff.

### 5.2 Build meaningful illustrated components

The kit should support the visible components required by that inventory, including the relevant head/face structure, hairstyles, color treatments, glasses, head accessories, garments, satchel/strap elements, and arm/hand gesture art.

These components must be authored specifically at the Harmony cut-in quality level. Do not simply enlarge the ordinary dialogue portrait’s layers.

Separate components according to customization and animation needs. A complete layer should include the drawing needed to remain convincing when another legitimate component is removed or replaced. A portrait of someone wearing glasses is not a glasses-free base merely because the frames can be hidden; the covered eye region must still be properly drawn.

### 5.3 Use pose-aware drawing and occlusion

Define appropriate layer relationships for each relevant pose. Back hair, far arm, torso, strap, neck, face, glasses, fringe, near hand, and foreground accessories do not have one universally correct stacking order.

A hand crossing a face can correctly cover part of the glasses; it must not cause the entire glasses layer to jump in front of the fingers or disappear. Straps follow the torso, sleeves follow the arms, and earrings or flowers remain attached to the correct side.

Where a turn changes visible anatomy or garment overlap, provide suitable authored pose art. Rotating flat pieces is acceptable only where the resulting movement remains convincing.

### 5.4 Preserve shading through color variation

Color variation must preserve the quality of the illustration. Use suitable material masks, authored color ramps, or equivalent controlled recoloring rather than indiscriminately tinting the entire portrait.

Changing the hair color should not recolor skin, glasses, or clothing. Changing a garment should not destroy its fold structure or introduce a different lighting direction. Darker and lighter supported colors must both retain readable form.

The exact recoloring implementation is a technical choice to document and demonstrate; a mask proposal alone is not proof that every supported palette looks good.

### 5.5 Preserve identity and current equipment

Read the effective worn appearance when the Harmony action begins. Preserve the actual hairstyle, colors, glasses state, garment shape, visible head accessories, and satchel/strap where applicable.

An item naturally outside the crop does not need to be shown. An item that should be visible cannot be omitted, substituted, or repositioned arbitrarily to simplify the artwork.

Do not hard-code the reference character’s flower, glasses, skin tone, or outfit into every player. Do not introduce an unrequested gendered redesign or other identity change.

### 5.6 Preserve assembly quality through animation

Use consistent component alignment, attachment points, and pose conventions across frames. Hair, facial features, garment edges, and accessories must not drift, change identity, or develop seams as the animation progresses.

Cache and reuse correctly assembled art where appropriate, but keep appearance and art-version changes from producing stale portraits. A portrait from a different campaign or previously equipped look must not appear in the next action.

## 6. Companion treatment

The companions have fixed identities, which makes their asset production more bounded. It does **not** remove their animation requirement.

Each companion needs high-fidelity, purpose-authored Harmony art, with a distinctive main pose and a short internal performance. A finished static picture may serve as a visual approval frame; it is not the finished animated companion cut-in.

Preserve the previously specified personalities and performances:

| Companion | Portrait performance direction |
|---|---|
| **Suzu** | Theatrical, confident, and welcoming to the shared spotlight. A lively head/hand flourish, knowing expression or single wink, and one well-timed glint may support the performance. Hair, ribbon, and earrings follow through naturally. |
| **Nao** | Practical, focused, and assured. A precise indicating gesture and a small confident shift should feel purposeful rather than showy. |
| **Mio** | Composed care and competence. A deliberate gesture with an established apothecary detail and a reassuring expression—not a generic timid pose. |
| **Ren** | Thoughtful, exact, and protective. A measured lamp/hand gesture and intent expression; a glasses adjustment alone cannot constitute the whole performance. |

These directions refine the existing Harmony presentation. They do not change technique effects, targeting, costs, or companion personality.

Both participants must receive comparable artistic care. Do not complete richly illustrated companions while leaving the player visibly in the simpler style.

## 7. Animation within the cut-in

### 7.1 One brief performance per activation

The paired cut-in must contain **internal character animation**, not only movement of the entire image layer.

Each legal Harmony activation receives one coordinated sequence: a quick left-edge entrance, a brief expressive beat, subtle follow-through, and a smooth fade in place. Do not loop the performance or repeat it for every affected target.

A fixed backing may move with the pair, but the characters must visibly perform within it.

### 7.2 Shared timing, distinct character contribution

The player can reuse an appropriate core rally animation across pairings. Each companion must retain a distinct animation based on their own gesture, expression, and secondary movement.

A color swap, different prop, or different playback speed on the same unchanged pose sequence does not establish companion-specific performance.

The entry, expression change, gesture, and fade should feel like one shared moment. Avoid two unrelated portrait animations competing for attention.

### 7.3 Keep the existing brief duration

Preserve the existing approved timing direction from the Harmony addendum. The correction is primarily about **fidelity, pose quality, and authored appeal**, not making the sequence substantially longer.

The flourish is part of the technique’s existing animation interval. Do not add a separate portrait cutscene before an otherwise unchanged full attack, and do not replay or replace good on-field choreography solely because the portraits are being revised.

Do not claim a specific frame count guarantees quality. Provide enough meaningful poses and transitions to make the chosen brief motion read cleanly.

### 7.4 Playback and accessibility

Normal shows the full intended short performance. Fast retains the identifying gesture and clear result while shortening the presentation. Instant omits the decorative cut-in rather than flashing it for a single frame. Reduced motion uses suitable held poses and restrained fades without sacrificing drawing quality.

Fade the portraits and their backing smoothly; do not abruptly erase them or slide them back out. Skip, interruption, scene exit, and campaign changes must clear the presentation safely without repeating combat effects.

## 8. Preserve what is already working

Keep the current correct trigger logic, compact left-side placement, customization resolution, animation ownership, menu behavior, banner ownership, and cleanup. Verify these against the newest implementation instead of assuming every detail in an older capture remains current.

The surrounding battle frame is not a canvas to redesign for this correction. Do not move the status dock, enlarge the overlay to consume the arena, shrink the enemy, reposition the party, or rearrange the background to compensate for weak portrait composition.

**Resolve/HP and Harmony remain visible.** Respect expanded or language-critical intentions, the active action banner, relevant enemy information, the on-field party performance, and essential controls. Preserve supported narrow-screen behavior and the existing safe-placement rules.

The top banner identifies **only the currently animated action**. It does not remain visible during decisions, reading, recognition correction, gaps between actions, victory gestures, or dialogue. The portrait is not an excuse to add another large “Harmony” heading or a second action title.

Retain all existing combat rules, learning behavior, input alternatives, saves, and offline/static delivery. Externally authored assets may be created during development, then incorporated through the project’s existing build. Do not add a runtime image-generation service or network requirement.

## 9. Unacceptable final resolutions

The following do not satisfy this correction:

| Proposed shortcut | Why it does not meet the target |
|---|---|
| Enlarge the current portraits or increase export resolution without changing their drawing | The requested difference is in authored form, expression, depth, and composition. |
| Add particles, glints, or noise around unchanged faces and poses | Decoration cannot substitute for the missing illustration quality. |
| Treat the selected mockup as placement-only inspiration | It is explicitly the fidelity reference too. |
| Preserve customization by keeping the player much simpler than the companion | Both requirements must be met together. |
| Replace the player with one fixed illustrated appearance | That removes an existing feature rather than solving the art problem. |
| Slide and fade otherwise motionless final portraits | The required internal animated flourish is missing. |
| Call the cut-ins final because they pass functional tests | Technical success and visual approval are separate. |
| Repeat “code cannot do this” without a concrete alternative production brief | A limitation of the attempted method calls for a specific asset workflow or honest blocker, not a lowered target. |

No blanket prohibition on code-authored art is imposed. Use the production method that demonstrably reaches the target. Equally, do not continue minor revisions of the same unsuccessful method indefinitely while describing the result as close enough.

If the required assets cannot be produced with available tools, identify the missing capability and prepare the actionable external-art handoff in Section 11. Keep the relevant visual item open rather than inventing completion.

## 10. Implementation and approval sequence

### Phase 1 — Establish the true visual quality bar

Create a revised **player + Suzu** composition directed closely at the selected mockup. This is the reference-quality test case.

Show the principal held pose at its intended in-battle size and at native authoring size, followed by its short internal animation and actual battle integration. Keep the comparison focused on the same visual footprint rather than gaining an apparent improvement through a larger overlay.

Request the owner’s visual approval before using this result as the production template for the entire roster. An internally assigned art score does not replace that approval.

### Phase 2 — Prove customization survives the upgrade

Demonstrate at least one **materially different supported player appearance** through the same system. It should exercise a different hairstyle and a different visible garment or accessory arrangement, not merely a recolored copy of the reference look.

Show the still and the animation. Verify that the alternate appearance retains the same face quality, coherent shading, layer alignment, and expressive energy.

This second example proves the approach; it does not reduce final support to two appearances.

### Phase 3 — Finalize the asset and integration contract

Once the quality direction and customization proof are accepted, formalize the real variant inventory, working dimensions, layer conventions, recoloring rules, pose dependencies, export process, registry mapping, and runtime integration.

Use or revise a handoff already being prepared rather than commissioning a second incompatible kit. Preserve approved source art and the means to regenerate its runtime exports.

### Phase 4 — Roll out to every companion and supported look

Produce Nao, Mio, and Ren at the same approved standard, preserving each companion’s distinctive performance. Complete the supported player components and test the relevant combinations.

A successful Suzu showcase is a milestone, not the whole assignment. Keep missing components and unapproved pairings visible in the requirement ledger.

### Phase 5 — Integrated acceptance

Demonstrate legal Harmony activations in the actual game, at normal display scale, with the final assembled artwork. Check the existing playback settings, protected screen regions, equipment changes, and interruption handling.

Preserve the project’s currently authorized wider validation order. Perform focused checks during this work; do not use a repeated whole-game matrix as a substitute for looking at the artwork, or as a reason to skip visual approval.

## 11. External-art assistance and the technical handoff

If outside-generated or externally authored assets are needed, prepare a **precise, source-grounded technical art brief**, not a general request for “better portraits.”

| Handoff item | Required information |
|---|---|
| **Source basis** | Current source revision or identifiable working-tree snapshot; authoritative appearance/equipment registries; relevant integration modules. |
| **Reference roles** | The chosen mockup for fidelity and composition; canonical character images for identity; current implementation samples for comparison rather than imitation. |
| **Asset inventory** | Exact supported component IDs, valid variations, visible equipment rules, and which appearances each requested asset covers. |
| **Canvas and display** | Working dimensions, intended on-screen size, crop rules, padding, coordinate origin, and common alignment convention. Distinguish authoring pixels from display pixels. |
| **Layer structure** | Components that must remain separate, required hidden artwork, pose-dependent ordering, attachment points, and occlusion/mask conventions. |
| **Color support** | Recolorable materials, authored palette or mask rules, protected regions, and supported color examples. |
| **Animation** | The shared timeline, character-specific cue, required key poses or frames, secondary-motion dependencies, and how the game controls playback. |
| **Transparency and export** | Real alpha requirements, edge treatment, expected formats, and runtime packaging. Do not accept a checkerboard painted into a flattened image as transparency. |
| **Naming and manifest** | File names, registry keys, frame/layer associations, required metadata, and how missing pieces are detected. |
| **Proof and delivery** | An assembled example, alternate-appearance example, in-context preview, editable/source components, and the actual integration steps. |

Do not invent registry values to fill the brief. Distinguish verified project constraints from proposed production choices and unresolved questions.

A flattened reference image is not already a separated animation kit. Asset creation, component separation, alignment, cleanup, recoloring, and animation continuity must be supplied and checked as real deliverables. Do not assume an image tool will automatically return production-ready layers or temporally consistent frames merely because a prompt requests them.

Do not require the outside contributor to reproduce the entire game. Ask for the smallest precise asset set that can establish the approved player-and-Suzu quality proof, then use the accepted contract to expand it.

The owner can relay that brief and return the resulting files. No external generation or background collaboration should be reported as underway until it actually is.

## 12. Final acceptance and delivery

Keep the following acceptance decisions distinct:

| Acceptance area | Required evidence |
|---|---|
| **Visual fidelity** | The owner accepts the candidate as materially close to the selected mockup in expression, hair, clothing, lighting, pose, and paired composition. |
| **Customization** | The effective worn look is represented accurately; a materially different appearance retains the same fidelity, followed by coverage of the actual supported inventory. |
| **Internal animation** | Each pairing performs a brief, unique character moment within the portraits; the player participates; the sequence is not merely a still image sliding and fading. |
| **Battle integration** | The compact overlay respects the existing frame, permanent status, intentions, banner, stage choreography, and playback controls. |
| **Rules and persistence** | No extra resource spending, effects, turns, rewards, eligibility changes, or save alterations result from the presentation. |
| **Production completeness** | Source assets, exports, assembly conventions, registry mapping, tests, and unresolved limitations are documented and preserved. |

Use explicit labels such as **provisional artwork**, **visual candidate awaiting approval**, **approved visual direction**, and **integrated and verified**. Do not describe an unapproved candidate as the finalized art merely because its implementation works.

Deliver the revised playable build through the established workflow, together with the source and art assets needed to continue development. Supply real in-engine evidence; do not substitute another generated mockup for a demonstrated implementation.

The final result should feel like a high-fidelity, expressive reward for the party’s coordination: compact, unmistakably cooperative, briefly animated, and fully compatible with the player’s chosen appearance.

> **The outcome is not “the best ordinary pixel portrait the current generator can produce.” It is an authored Harmony illustration system that preserves customization and lands very close to the chosen mockup’s style, detail, and impact.**
